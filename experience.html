A COURSE THAT EXPANDED MY HORIZONS

I enrolled in ITEC 101 because someone I trusted thought I'd find it interesting. I didn't put a whole lot of thought into it - I just knew that I was open to something else outside of the business school.

ITEC 101 introduced me to the fundamentals of information technology - systems thinking, data structures, the architecture of how digital tools actually function beneath the interface. For a Finance and Supply Chain student, I found it to be fascinating. Operations is, at its core, an information problem. Supply chains live and die by data flow. There were many parallels between the language of IT and supply chain.

What stood out most wasn't any single lecture, but rather the quality of the learning environment. My professor brought a mastery to the material based on his own professional experience. It was the kind of class where you left feeling like you'd actually been sharpened, not just informed. By the end of the semester, I had declared an Information Technology minor. That's not something I planned. That's something ITEC 101 earned.

The connection to my major and career goals became clearer the more I sat with it. Wealth management is increasingly a technology-informed field. Client data management, portfolio analytics, digital advisory tools - understanding the infrastructure behind those systems isn't a nice-to-have anymore. ITEC 101 gave me a head start on that fluency, and the minor I'm building out is a direct extension of what that course opened up.

03 — Beyond the Classroom

FROM PROSPECTIVE STUDENT TO AMBASSADOR

I remember the ambassador who gave me a tour of the Moore School when I was still a prospective student. I don't remember every word they said, but I remember how I felt afterward. At the time, it was an odd feeling... I had essentially written USC off and placed it at the bottom of my college list, but I began to wonder if Carolina was really the place where I belonged. That feeling played a role in my college decision. So when I saw the application for the Ambassador Program in my first year, it felt like the perfect opportunity to provide an authentic perspective to other future USC students.

I was selected in the spring of my freshman year to join the program. But before I could hold the title and give tours, we had to go through an intensive training process. The job itself seemed pretty straightforward on the surface: lead tours, represent the school, field questions from families. But as I came to realize, it's much more about creating relationships and connections. Not just with prospective students, but also with faculty, staff, and everyone else in the business school. That being said, every conversation with a prospective student is a chance to tell the truth about what DMSB is like, and to do it with enough specificity and warmth that families leave with something real. I've led over 100 personalized tours. Each one is different.

This past spring, my role expanded significantly. I helped lead the interviewing process for the next class of ambassadors - narrowing a pool of 180+ applicants down to a selective cohort of 15. Since then, I've been promoted to assistant director, where I help with scheduling, coordinate events with faculty and staff, and play an active role in shaping the culture of the program going forward.

What makes this experience meaningful to me isn't the promotion or the resume line. It's the continuity. Someone did this for me, and now I get to do it for someone else. That loop - of being well-served and then turning around to serve well - is something I want to carry into every professional context I enter.

04 — Connection
ITEC 101 ↔ Ambassador Program

Service isn't a soft skill.
It's a system.

On the surface, an IT course and a student ambassador role don't have much in common. The thread that pulls them together for me showed up somewhere I didn't expect: working the Masters Tournament at Augusta National.

Augusta isn't just a golf tournament. It's one of the most meticulously managed operational environments in the world - high volume, high expectation, zero tolerance for friction. I processed over 100 patrons per hour and $10K in daily sales. Standing in that flow, I started seeing the week the way ITEC 101 had trained me to see any system: inputs, processes, outputs, and the information moving underneath every interaction. Every handoff, every bottleneck, every moment something either worked or broke down - that was systems thinking under pressure, not abstract theory from a lecture.

That same lens followed me back into the Ambassador Program. Tours stopped looking like isolated conversations and started looking like a sequence of touchpoints in a larger experience. Consistency isn't just courtesy - it's reliability in a system. When families walk through the Moore School, they deserve the same intentionality Augusta demands: clear information, smooth process, and care at every step.

When I stepped into the assistant director role and began shaping scheduling and culture, I was thinking about the program the way ITEC 101 taught me to think about technology - where are the friction points, how do we standardize what works, and how do we build something that runs well even when I'm not in the room? The classroom gave me the mental models. Masters showed me what those models look like at scale. The Ambassador role gave me a place to use them.

That's the real connection. Not that these experiences happened at the same time, but that ITEC 101 changed how I see service itself - as a system you can design, refine, and run with intention - and Masters proved why that mindset matters.
